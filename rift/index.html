Dossier · The Rift

What the Rift is

Nobody in this setting knows. That is not a gap in the worldbuilding — it is the worldbuilding. The Rift is written as an event that three civilisations interpreted through their own politics, and every interpretation is in the published record as a claim rather than a fact.

The physical account is short and consistent across sources. On 6 February 1944 the United States detonated the Trinity device at Los Alamos, and the detonation opened a tear in space and time. Within a month an atomic bomb destroyed Dresden and a second tear opened in the crater. Two Rifts, both open, both permanent, both still there in 1947.

Everything else is consequence. Rift-tech is what happens when engineers get access to a phenomenon they cannot model: they build around the parts that repeat. Walkers work, so walkers are built. The serum works, so the serum is refined. The dead come back, so the dead are issued uniforms. None of this requires understanding, which is precisely why it spreads.

The setting’s own narrators are unreliable by design. A Neu Republik minister describes Dresden as an inheritance. An American president describes Los Alamos as a commission from God. A Soviet commissar describes the Science Cities as producing heroes. An emperor describes his laboratories as a necessary answer. Each is a claim about what the Rift is, made by someone who benefits from that answer.

Three readings

The same event,
three doctrines

Reading I · Occult

The Green Vault

The Rift is a door that was always there, and the detonations were merely the correct knock. This is the Vault’s position, and it is why their machines carry the names of European gods: Wotan, Thor, the Nachtjäger, the Nachtalben. Technology is liturgy. The dead who rise are not anomalies but evidence.

Reading II · Scientific

The United States · the Commonwealth

The Rift is a physical phenomenon with exploitable properties: an energy gradient, a topology, a resource. This reading produced Tesla weapons, walkers and automation, and it is the reading that treats the dead as a materials-handling problem. It is also the reading that cannot explain why the President came back.

Reading III · Nihilist

The Soviet Bloc · Imperial Japan

The Rift is a wound in a world that was already dying, and the only rational response is to take as much as possible before it closes or widens. Soviet salvage science and the Japanese phase programmes both proceed from this premise. Neither power has a Rift of its own; both have decided that ownership is a formality.

The mechanic

Borrowed power,
written as a rule

Konflikt ’47 encodes its central argument in the Rift Die. Every Rift unit contributes one; the die is drawn from the same bag as Order Dice and assigned to a Rift unit; and its state — Exhausted, Active or Surging — decides what the technology does.

A surging Tesla cannon gains additional shots, better accuracy against vehicles, and ignores cover. A surging Stahltruppen unit passes its order automatically and discards its pins — and then, exhausted, is slowed for the rest of the turn. Power is real, temporary, and paid for.

Heroes interact with the system directly. Rift Mastery lets a character hold an extra die, feed dice to nearby units, or combine two Active dice into one Surging die. In a setting about people who reach into something they do not understand, the characters are the ones allowed to reach furthest.
